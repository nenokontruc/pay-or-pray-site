When does freemium make sense?

Most of today's biggest mobile games are free to download — yet they earn billions. This project explains how, and asks one question: when does “free” actually pay off? The short answer depends on one thing most people never notice: whether players know exactly what they're buying, or are paying for a chance at it.

Deterministic

Fixed Price

Like buying a specific trading card from a shop: you see it, you pay, you get it. Players compare options and choose.

Probabilistic

Random Draw

Like buying a sealed pack of cards: you pay first and find out what's inside after. Luck, near-misses, and “just one more” drive spending.

New here? The 60-second version

No gaming or pricing background needed. Four ideas are enough to follow everything on this site.

1

Freemium

“Free + premium.” The game is free to play; the company earns money by selling optional extras inside it. Most players never pay — a small share pays a lot.

2

Gacha / loot box

A paid lucky draw inside a game. You spend money for a chance at a rare item. Named after Japanese capsule-toy machines.

3

Pay-to-win

When money buys a real competitive advantage, not just a new look. Players who don't pay often feel this is unfair — and leave.

4

Willingness to pay

The most a person would pay for something. It differs between people, and between countries — which is why one global price rarely works.

The argument in one sentence: selling a known item and selling a lucky draw are two different pricing problems — they need different tools, carry different risks, and players in different countries react to them differently. The rest of the site shows the evidence.

Industry

The market, its models, and where the rules are headed

Four things worth knowing before the case study: how large this market is, how freemium got here, the full menu of ways a free game makes money, and the regulatory patchwork that happens to be the reason exact gacha odds are even public.

The market this argument sits inside

Mobile gaming is the largest single platform in the games industry, and freemium is why — nearly every top-grossing mobile title today is free to download. But the market is also showing its first signs of maturing.

Three publishers anchor almost every example in this project. Tencent — the world's largest games company by revenue — publishes Honor of Kings and PUBG Mobile. NetEase, China's second-largest publisher, backs Everstone Studio's Where Winds Meet. HoYoverse (miHoYo) publishes Genshin Impact — not a case study here, for reasons the Case Study page explains.

2025 was also the first year non-gaming apps out-earned games in in-app purchase revenue globally — a quiet signal that the mobile games market, after a decade of growth, is starting to plateau just as the companies in this study are testing the limits of what freemium pricing can still extract.

How freemium got here

Freemium wasn’t invented by mobile games. It moved through three eras — and each one changed what a pricing manager has to decide.

Origins of the modelMobile scaleRegulation & maturity
  1. 1980s
    Software is given away to sell the upgrade

    Shareware programs spread for free and asked users to pay for the full version. The model existed decades before it had a name.

    Pricing lesson: free is a distribution strategy, not a price.
  2. 1999
    Games go free-to-play

    Nexon’s QuizQuiz in South Korea became one of the first online games to be free to play and earn from selling in-game items.

    Pricing lesson: revenue shifts from one sale per player to many small sales to a few players.
  3. 2006
    The model gets its name

    Venture capitalist Fred Wilson described giving a service away and charging for premium features, and asked readers to name it. Jarid Lukin of Alacra suggested “freemium.”

    Pricing lesson: the free tier is the customer-acquisition engine.
  4. 2009
    Apple lets free apps sell inside the app

    In October 2009, Apple allowed in-app purchases in free iPhone apps, which had previously been limited to paid apps — opening mobile to the free-to-play model.

    Pricing lesson: the price ladder moves inside the game, and platform price tiers start to shape it.
  5. 2012
    Japan bans one gacha design

    Japan’s Consumer Affairs Agency moved against “complete gacha” — draws that reward collecting a full random set — while ordinary gacha stayed legal.

    Pricing lesson: regulators judge how a lucky draw is designed, not just whether one exists.
  6. 2015
    Honor of Kings launches

    Tencent’s MOBA, monetized through skins, grows into China’s highest-grossing mobile game — this project’s first case.

    Pricing lesson: cosmetic-first selling can work at enormous scale.
  7. 2017–18
    Disclosure and backlash

    China required games to publish drop rates (2017). After the Star Wars Battlefront II controversy, Belgium declared paid loot boxes illegal gambling in April 2018.

    Pricing lesson: probability becomes a disclosed — and legally risky — part of the price.
  8. 2024–25
    A maturing market

    Where Winds Meet launches in China (December 2024) with a cosmetic-only gacha. In 2025, non-game apps out-earn games in in-app purchases for the first time.

    Pricing lesson: growth now comes from smarter design and localization, not from more players.

Two economies, not one

Pricing an item isn't just about its role inside the game. An industry practitioner interviewed for this project draws a sharp line between two distinct pricing problems a publisher solves at once.

In-Game Economy

Supply vs. demand, inside the game

Items with scarce supply but high use-demand command higher value; items with abundant gameplay supply should be cheap or free, to keep progression from stalling.

Out-Game Economy

Purchasing power, across markets

The same item needs a different price in different markets — not a currency conversion, but a localization of relative value, sized to what that market can actually bear.

The localization problem is concrete, not abstract. For one evergreen package line spanning 6–8 price tiers, the practitioner's own benchmark: the best-selling tier in a mature Western market lands around US$10–19, while the best-selling tier for the same package line in Vietnam lands closer to US$5–7 — roughly half. A publisher that ports its US price ladder unchanged into a new market isn't neutral; it's mispriced for that market's actual willingness-to-pay on one side or the other.

The dashed line marks a single flat $15 price — inside the US-like range, well above the VN-like one. This is the exact mismatch the Simulation lets you create and feel the revenue cost of.

This is precisely the variable the Simulation on this site now lets you test directly — pick a target market, then watch whether your pricing was actually calibrated to it or just carried over from habit.

Framework

Two formulas, one variable

The course's own EVC formula is the pricing ceiling for any good. Whether it applies directly, or has to be re-derived in expectation, depends entirely on whether the good itself is certain or left to chance.

Deterministic goodsApplies directly
EVC = RefP + DiffV
The maximum defensible price: the price of the next-best alternative, plus the differentiated value this item adds over it. Both terms are known quantities when the good itself is certain — as with a skin bought directly from the shop.
RefP — reference price of the next-best alternative
DiffV — differentiated value over that alternative
Probabilistic goodsConsistent with published OR literature
EVC(gacha) = P(success) × Value − P(failure) × Cost
A reframing of the same course formula for a good whose delivery is uncertain: expected gain from success, minus the expected sunk cost of failing. Asked whether EVC can be applied to gaming at all, guest speaker Dr. Tim Smith said yes — but that it is trickier than a standard case. The general form below, from the operations research literature on loot box design, supports the structure: expected value is a drop-rate-weighted sum of marginal value across every possible outcome, not just a binary success/failure — this simplified two-outcome version is the special case relevant to chasing one specific item.
V(A,v,r) = Σⱼ rⱼ × [v(A∪{j}) − v(A)]
Expected utility of a loot box, summed over every possible item j: its drop rate rⱼ, times the marginal value it adds given the items (A) the player already owns — so a duplicate of something already owned contributes zero. — Han, Ryan & Tong, 2026

The rest of the toolkit

Willingness-to-pay (WTP). The course's price-response function, d(p) = D·(1 − W(p)), defines Paying Rate as P(wtp ≥ p) — the share of players whose willingness-to-pay for an item exceeds its price. Paying Rate is low not because prices are high, but because most players’ willingness to pay for extras is zero — the free game is enough. Newzoo estimates that 56% of all players spend nothing on games in a year, so the share paying in any single game is a minority. Low entry prices exist to convert the marginal player; ARPPU (average spend among payers) can still be very high.

Choice Architecture. Compromise and anchor effects require a small set of known-value alternatives to compare — exactly the setting a fixed-price shop creates. A gacha purchase has no such comparison set; the relevant psychology shifts to near-miss effects, sunk-cost escalation, and pity-timer anchoring instead. This is the mechanism this project's case study is built to isolate.

02 — Where the randomness ends

Same idea, opposite designs

Both draws are (near-)cosmetic and both guarantee a prize eventually. The differences are in how the odds move, what you win, and what you pay with.

Honor of Kings · 积分夺宝Where Winds Meet · Echo gacha
Starting odds0.8% for the Honor Crystal0.0415% for the featured outfit
How odds changeRise with a “luck value” on every drawFixed until the guarantee
Guarantee361 draws (360 in the limited version)150 pulls, shared across both banners
What you winA crystal you exchange for the collector skin you chooseOne specific outfit the studio chose

Pay-to-winNegligible — many skins add small stat bonuses (e.g. +10 attack)None in either channel
The key difference: in Honor of Kings, chance decides how much you pay, but you choose what you get — the lottery turns back into a known purchase at the end. In Where Winds Meet, chance decides both how much you pay and, until the guarantee, whether you get the item at all.

03 — What this means for pricing

The item, not the game, sets the pricing tool

The fixed-price skins in both games are priced with the standard EVC formula: the value of the item is known before purchase, so compromise effects, anchors, and tiered ladders do the work.

Honor of Kings' draw is close to deterministic at the end. Because players pick the final skin, the uncertainty is mostly about price — somewhere between 1 and 361 draws — not about value. And because much of the draw currency builds up as a side effect of spending, the lottery works more like a loyalty rebate layered on the shop than a standalone product.

Where Winds Meet's draw is fully probabilistic until the 150-pull guarantee: both the cost and the outcome are uncertain, so the expected-value form of EVC on the Framework page is the one that applies.

That is why this project compares what is being sold, not whole games. The same storefront can need two different pricing tools.

A guaranteed prize matters economically, not just for goodwill. It moves a draw toward the “unique box” design, where every purchase yields an item the player doesn’t already own. In Chen et al.’s model, unique boxes approach the full revenue-optimal ceiling as the catalog grows, while duplicate-allowing boxes capture only about 36.7% of it. — Chen, Elmachtoub, Hamilton & Lei, 2021
Staying cosmetic-only has a revenue cost: Wuthering Waves, which puts power-affecting characters in its gacha, earned roughly $320M over 21 months — nearly double Where Winds Meet's pace on a broadly comparable download base. — Naavik, 2026

Why this pairing, specifically

Almost every top-grossing game sells both fixed-price items and lucky draws, so no clean “deterministic game vs. probabilistic game” pair exists. What can be held (approximately) constant is power: Where Winds Meet sells none in any channel, and Honor of Kings’ skins add only small stat bonuses — small enough that academic work on the game treats skins as not affecting match outcomes. That isolates the design of the lucky draw itself as the main variable being compared. This near-match on power is a limitation worth stating, not a perfect control.

Genshin Impact was considered and rejected. It also sells both, but its draws grant characters and weapons that make players stronger — so any difference could come from the randomness or from the power, not cleanly from either.

Diagnostic Framework

Where any freemium good lands

Two questions place any purchasable good in one of four risk positions — before a single price is set.

DETERMINISTIC VALUE
PROBABILISTIC VALUE
COSMETIC / NON-POWER
Lowest risk

Fixed-price cosmetic shop

e.g. the skin shops in Honor of Kings and Where Winds Meet. Compromise/anchor Choice Architecture. The recommended default for most titles.

Moderate risk

Cosmetic lucky draw

e.g. HoK’s Points Treasure Hunt and WWM’s Echo gacha. Pity/near-miss Choice Architecture. Pay-to-win-free, but the probability mechanic alone still draws regulatory attention.

POWER-AFFECTING
High risk

Fixed pay-to-win shop

Fairness backlash well documented in player-perception research. Generally advised against.

Highest risk

Power-affecting loot boxes

Combines gambling-adjacent mechanics with competitive unfairness — the primary target of current loot-box regulation.

Reading the matrix

The diagonal from top-left to bottom-right is the one that matters most: it runs from the safest commercial position (known goods, no power) to the position regulators are actively targeting (chance-based goods that also confer competitive advantage). Both case-study games straddle the top row: their skin shops sit in the top-left cell (Honor of Kings right at its lower edge, given its small skin stat bonuses), and their lucky draws sit one cell over, in moderate risk — because removing power from a probabilistic mechanic reduces but does not eliminate its regulatory exposure. Honor of Kings' draw sits closer to the left edge of that cell than Where Winds Meet's, because players choose the final prize.

Freemium "makes sense," in the terms this project set out to answer, when a design deliberately stays in the top row — and when, if it does use a probabilistic mechanic, the Choice Architecture and EVC model applied to it are the ones built for chance, not the ones built for certainty.

How costly each cell is also depends on the market. Players in China have historically accepted paid power far more than Western players, so the same item can sit in a more dangerous position in the US than in China — a difference the Simulation lets you test.

How this simulation works
A panel of simulated monthly active players (1,000 at launch, each representing 200 real players — established players, not raw installs). Most of them (72–82%, depending on market) never spend at any price, matching the freemium pattern that most players don’t pay; each with a persistent spending segment, daily engagement rate and a monthly budget that all packages share. Each month is simulated day by day. Players churn more when goodwill falls below 60; new installs arrive faster when goodwill is high. A player buys the highest price tier they can afford; gacha players pull until they win or hit the 50-pull pity, limited by budget.
Offering more than 6 tiers in one ladder slightly lowers conversion (choice overload; directionally after Iyengar & Lepper, 2000). Survey answers are inflated ~1.35× to reflect hypothetical bias in stated willingness to pay (Murphy et al., 2005).
