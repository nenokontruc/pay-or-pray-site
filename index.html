Industry

The market, its models, and where the rules are headed

Three things worth knowing before the case study: how large and concentrated this market is, the full menu of ways a free game makes money, and the regulatory patchwork that happens to be the reason exact gacha odds are even public.

The market this argument sits inside

Mobile gaming is the largest single platform in the games industry, and freemium is why — nearly every top-grossing mobile title today is free to download. But the market is also showing its first signs of maturing.

Three publishers anchor almost every example in this project. Tencent — the world's largest games company by revenue — publishes Honor of Kings and PUBG Mobile. NetEase, China's second-largest publisher, backs Everstone Studio's Where Winds Meet. HoYoverse (miHoYo) publishes Genshin Impact — not a case study here, for reasons the diagnostic page explains.

2025 was also the first year non-gaming apps out-earned games in in-app purchase revenue globally — a quiet signal that the mobile games market, after a decade of growth, is starting to plateau just as the companies in this study are testing the limits of what freemium pricing can still extract.

Framework

Two formulas, one variable

The course's own EVC formula is the pricing ceiling for any good. Whether it applies directly, or has to be re-derived in expectation, depends entirely on whether the good itself is certain or left to chance.

Deterministic goodsApplies directly
EVC = RefP + DiffV
The maximum defensible price: the price of the next-best alternative, plus the differentiated value this item adds over it. Both terms are known quantities when the good itself is certain — as with every Honor of Kings skin.
RefP — reference price of the next-best alternative
DiffV — differentiated value over that alternative
Probabilistic goodsPending full validation
EVC(gacha) = P(success) × Value − P(failure) × Cost
A reframing of the same course formula for a good whose delivery is uncertain: expected gain from success, minus the expected sunk cost of failing. Guest speaker Dr. Tim Smith confirmed this direction is workable but flagged it as trickier than a standard EVC case — he pointed to the 5-Hour ENERGY pricing case as a useful reference before this is finalized.

The rest of the toolkit

Willingness-to-pay (WTP). The course's price-response function, d(p) = D·(1 − W(p)), defines Paying Rate as P(wtp ≥ p) — the share of players whose willingness-to-pay for an item exceeds its price. Most freemium titles set prices low relative to the bulk of this distribution, which is why Paying Rate is typically in the single digits while ARPPU (average spend among payers) can be very high.

Choice Architecture. Compromise and anchor effects require a small set of known-value alternatives to compare — exactly the setting a fixed-price shop creates. A gacha purchase has no such comparison set; the relevant psychology shifts to near-miss effects, sunk-cost escalation, and pity-timer anchoring instead. This is the mechanism this project's case study is built to isolate.

Case Study

Same storefront logic, two Chinese publishers

Honor of Kings sells nothing but certainty. Where Winds Meet sells both certainty and chance, in the same shop, to the same players — which is what makes it possible to isolate determinism as a variable rather than just describe two different games.

A cosmetic-only "fair gacha" has a real revenue cost: Wuthering Waves, which gates power-affecting characters behind its gacha, earned roughly $320M over 21 months — nearly double Where Winds Meet's pace on a broadly comparable download base. Removing pay-to-win content removes a meaningful share of a gacha system's revenue-extraction power. — Naavik, 2026

Why this pairing, specifically

The original probabilistic case for this project was Genshin Impact — rejected because its gacha grants gameplay power (characters, weapons), not just cosmetics. That confounds two variables at once: any difference observed could be attributed to either the probabilistic mechanic or the power stakes, not cleanly to determinism alone.

Where Winds Meet solves this by running both a deterministic (direct purchase) and a probabilistic (gacha) channel, side by side, in the same cosmetic-only storefront. That holds "non-power-affecting" constant and isolates determinism as the only variable that changes — the cleanest natural experiment available for this comparison.

Diagnostic Framework

Where any freemium good lands

Two questions place any purchasable good in one of four risk positions — before a single price is set.

DETERMINISTIC VALUE
PROBABILISTIC VALUE
COSMETIC / NON-POWER
Lowest risk

Honor of Kings archetype

Compromise/anchor Choice Architecture. The recommended default for most titles.

Moderate risk

Where Winds Meet gacha channel

Pity/near-miss Choice Architecture. Pay-to-win-free, but the probability mechanic alone still draws regulatory attention.

POWER-AFFECTING
High risk

Fixed pay-to-win shop

Fairness backlash well documented in player-perception research. Generally advised against.

Highest risk

Power-affecting loot boxes

Combines gambling-adjacent mechanics with competitive unfairness — the primary target of current loot-box regulation.

Reading the matrix

The diagonal from top-left to bottom-right is the one that matters most: it runs from the safest commercial position (known goods, no power) to the position regulators are actively targeting (chance-based goods that also confer competitive advantage). Honor of Kings sits entirely in the top-left cell. Where Winds Meet's direct-purchase channel sits there too — its gacha channel sits one cell over, in moderate risk, precisely because removing power from a probabilistic mechanic reduces but does not eliminate its regulatory exposure.

Freemium "makes sense," in the terms this project set out to answer, when a design deliberately stays in the top row — and when, if it does use a probabilistic mechanic, the Choice Architecture and EVC model applied to it are the ones built for chance, not the ones built for certainty.
